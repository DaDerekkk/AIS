###### Abstract

AI agents are vulnerable to indirect prompt injection attacks, where malicious instructions embedded in external content or tool outputs cause unintended or harmful behavior. Inspired by the well-established concept of firewalls, we show that a simple, modular, and model-agnostic defense operating at the agent–tool interface achieves perfect security with high utility across all four public benchmarks: AgentDojo, Agent Security Bench, InjecAgent and \tau-Bench, while achieving a state-of-the-art security-utility tradeoff compared to prior results. Specifically, we employ two firewalls: a Tool-Input Firewall (Minimizer) and a Tool-Output Firewall (Sanitizer). Unlike prior complex approaches, this defense makes minimal assumptions about the agent and can be deployed out of the box. This makes it highly generalizable while maintaining strong performance without compromising utility. Our analysis also reveals critical limitations in these existing benchmarks, including flawed success metrics, implementation bugs, and most importantly, weak attacks, hindering progress. To address this, we present targeted fixes to these issues for AgentDojo and Agent Security Bench, and propose best practices for more robust benchmark design. Moreover, we introduce a three-stage attack strategy that cascades standard prompt injection attacks, second-order attacks, and adaptive attacks to evaluate the robustness beyond existing attacks. Overall, our work shows that existing agentic security benchmarks are easily saturated by a simple approach and highlights the need for stronger benchmarks with carefully chosen evaluation metrics and strong adaptive attacks.

## 1 Introduction

LLMs are increasingly deployed as tool-calling agents that can browse the web, operate databases, and invoke external APIs (anthropic2024claude35; llama2024modelcards; openai2024functioncalling; yang2025qwen3; bahdanau2024tapeagents). This capability unlocks powerful applications like booking travel and paying bills but also expands the attack manifold. These agents are susceptible to malicious adversaries that can embed hidden instructions in seemingly benign external content (e.g., a web page, calendar entry, email, or database field), known as indirect prompt injection (IPI) attacks (zhang2025agentASB; debenedetti2024agentdojo; ruan2024identifying; liu2024formalizing; zhan-etal-2024-injecagent). When the agent reads this content, it may follow the attacker’s instructions rather than the user’s intent and can leak private information or carry out malicious actions. Hence, securing such systems is crucial, since even a single successful injection can cause real-world harm.

Existing defenses for IPI attacks largely rely on LLM retraining (wallace2024instruction; chen2025struq), LLM-based detectors (protectai_deberta_prompt_injection_2024), prompt-augmentation defenses (sandwich_defense_2023; hines2024defending) or system-level policies (Debenedetti2025CaMeL). Drawing from recent defenses inspired by the well-established concept of cybersecurity firewalls (BagdasarianAirGapAgent; abdelnabi2025firewalls), we investigate two complementary and lightweight LLM firewalls placed at the agent–tool boundary: a Tool-Input Firewall (Minimizer) and a Tool-Output Firewall (Sanitizer). The Minimizer mitigates data stealing attacks by minimizing any unnecessary data or private information from tool call arguments. The Sanitizer filters tool responses before they are fed back into the agent, removing suspicious instructions and potentially malicious content. Our experiments demonstrate that this simple “minimize & sanitize” defense that requires no LLM retraining or proprietary guardrails can achieve \sim 0% attack success with minimal utility degradation across four widely used benchmarks.

Benchmarking is critical for understanding and comparing the security of tool-calling agents. As attacks evolve and defenses adapt, researchers need consistent, realistic, and reproducible evaluation frameworks to identify true progress and avoid misleading conclusions. Several recent benchmarks, such as AgentDojo (debenedetti2024agentdojo), Agent Security Bench (zhang2025agentASB), and InjecAgent (zhan-etal-2024-injecagent), aim to simulate real-world attack scenarios. However, our analysis reveals that many benchmarks do not model real-world situations appropriately and sometimes employ skewed metrics to gauge performance. In such cases, even weak defenses may seem deceptively effective. We highlight these limitations and fix them through our proposed standardized benchmarking best practices.

Beyond targeted fixes for the existing benchmarks, we propose a much stronger attack strategy. We propose a three-stage cascaded attack strategy that escalates only when earlier attempts fail. The attacks progress from existing formatted injections to second-order semantic deception templates and finally utilize a defense-aware adaptive LLM mutator that iteratively refines attacks using feedback.

Our main contributions and findings are as follows:

*   •
We investigate a simple, effective, modular and model-agnostic defense for tool-calling agents based on two firewalls placed at the LLM–tool boundary: a Tool-Input Firewall (Minimizer) and a Tool-Output Firewall (Sanitizer). Together, they implement a “minimize & sanitize” principle to block IPI attacks without requiring any retraining and can be seamlessly integrated into any existing pipeline (Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")).

*   •
We demonstrate that this approach achieves the best security–utility tradeoff on all four public benchmarks: AgentDojo, Agent Security Bench, InjecAgent and \tau-bench. This highlights the generalizability of our approach without any performance degradation.

*   •
We identify key flaws in widely popular benchmarks (AgentDojo, Agent Security Bench, InjecAgent), propose targeted fixes, and release their corrected versions along with updated baseline results to enable more faithful and reliable evaluation.

*   •
We introduce a much stronger, three-stage cascade attack strategy that escalates from existing formatted injections, to second-order semantic deception templates, and finally to a defense-aware adaptive LLM mutator. This protocol surfaces failure modes missed by static benchmark attacks and provides a stronger, more realistic robustness evaluation.

## 2 Related Works

Recent efforts to improve agent security have focused on various ways of isolating one (or several) tool-calling LLMs, including execution isolation (Wu2025IsolateGPT; shi2025progent), restricted interpreters (Debenedetti2025CaMeL), and privilege-based information control (wu2024system; kim2025prompt). These “system-level” security methods often rely on a dual-LLM approach, leveraging quarantined agents with restricted actions that interface with untrusted sources, and a privileged agent that can interact with trusted users. While effective in preventing injection attacks, these approaches often incur a heavy implementation burden, such as in the case of CaMeL (Debenedetti2025CaMeL), which relies on a custom Python interpreter that constrains agents based on domain-specific policies. Further, increased security by CaMeL is also accompanied by reduced task success rates, an undesirable consequence.

Relatedly, BagdasarianAirGapAgent relies on an isolated LLM to perform context minimization for a secondary conversational LLM. abdelnabi2025firewalls extend this approach to multi-turn conversations, and introduce the use of “firewalled” agents. This involves orchestrating three policy-adhering LLM agents: an input firewall that sanitizes data input to the primary agent into a task-specific schema, a data firewall that minimizes user data, and a trajectory firewall that inspects intermediate agent steps. Our work simplifies this approach and demonstrates that a single output firewall is able to reliably prevent essentially all injection attacks across four agentic benchmark suites, without requiring task-specific policies or guidelines.

Additional injection defenses include repeating user prompts (sandwich_defense_2023), delimiting tool outputs with special characters (hines2024defending), filtering tools (debenedetti2024agentdojo), employing additional models to detect prompt injections (protectai_deberta_prompt_injection_2024; liu2025datasentinel; jacob2025promptshield), or trajectory re-execution (zhu2025melon). Our results demonstrate that these approaches are either ineffective at preventing attacks or do so at significant costs to task utility. Additionally, PromptArmor (shi2025promptarmorsimpleeffectiveprompt) utilizes an LLM to detect the injections and then remove the detected injections within tool calls using fuzzy logic. However, they employ distinct system messages for different benchmarks, which limits their generalizability. Another set of approaches, which rely on (re)training LLMs for improved robustness to attacks (wallace2024instruction; chen2025struq; chen2025secalign) require considerable resources and may not be compatible with black-box models. Thus, we do not ablate these methods, but consider them part of a complementary line of research.

Numerous benchmarks and evaluation suites have been developed to assess performance on defending against injection attacks (debenedetti2024agentdojo; zhan-etal-2024-injecagent; zhang2025agentASB). While these capture different facets of agent security, we highlight numerous shortcomings and areas for improvement. This includes implementation issues, bugs, design choices, and insufficient evaluation metrics. We also show that our relatively simple firewall defense is able to effectively eliminate attacks across all benchmarks with zero tuning, thus emphasizing the need for newer and more challenging security benchmarks. To this end, we also propose a stronger three-stage cascade attack strategy.

## 3 Method

Task Setup.  We assess the security risks posed by IPI attacks on LLM-based tool-calling agents tasked with multi-step user requests. In addition to the user task, the agent has access to a suite of tools for interacting with an environment (e.g., querying databases). To complete the user task, the agent may need to invoke multiple tools, provide each tool with the correct input arguments 1 1 1 This can also be \emptyset, i.e., no arguments are needed, and then use the resulting observations either to subsequent tool calls or return the final answer to the user.

Threat Model.  We consider attacks where adversaries may inject manipulative instructions or malicious content into the tool observations returned to the agent, intending to coerce the agent into executing an attacker-chosen objective. Injections may be appended to the tool response and exploit the agent’s reliance on untrusted content that can appear benign yet include hidden imperatives (e.g., “ignore previous instructions"). Such attacks can originate from various sources in the environment, such as a tool querying a compromised database, reading a malicious email, or calling unsafe third-party APIs. They may seek to accomplish various harmful actions, such as exfiltrating sensitive data or making fraudulent purchases. We focus on IPI attacks where the user is assumed benign and provides a safe and legitimate task. Finally, we assume no a priori knowledge of which tools are compromised or whether an injection is present, and therefore treat every tool output as potentially adversarial.

Defense Objective.  A defense should neutralize malicious tool outputs to prevent completion of the attacker’s objective, while preserving successful completion of the benign user task. Importantly, these objectives should remain aligned, and efforts to mitigate attack success rates (ASR) should not come at the cost of reduced utility, which would render the agent impractical in real-world deployment. In predominantly secure environments, IPI defenses should allow agents to exhibit strong benign utility (BU) (i.e. , utility in the absence of attacks). In untrusted environments (e.g., the open Web), defenses should still enable strong utility under attack (UA).

Firewall Defenses.  We introduce a Tool-Input Firewall (Minimizer) F_{I}, which removes unnecessary confidential information from tool input arguments before a tool is invoked. To decide what to retain, F_{I} is provided the trusted user task, the tool description, and the original tool arguments (full prompt in Appendix [B](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A2 "Appendix B Minimizer and Sanitizer Prompts ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")), since some workflows legitimately require private information. With F_{I} in place, only the filtered arguments are passed to the tool.

We also introduce a Tool-Output Firewall (Sanitizer) F_{O}, which sanitizes each returned tool observation to remove potentially malicious content. This firewall is provided with the original trusted user task (full prompt in Appendix [B](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A2 "Appendix B Minimizer and Sanitizer Prompts ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")). To mitigate injection attacks, every tool response is first passed through F_{O}, and only the sanitized content is forwarded to the primary LLM agent.

Unlike other guardrail approaches that merely flag suspected injections, this Minimizer-Sanitizer defense acts as a limited-privilege LLM that can selectively filter malicious content. We summarize the overall procedure in Algorithm [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3 Method ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?").

Algorithm 1 Input & Output Firewalls Pipeline

0: User task U, LLM Agent \mathcal{A}, Tool-Input Firewall F_{I}, Tool-Output Firewall F_{O}, Tools \mathcal{T}

0: Injection attacks do not succeed

1:Input: User provides trusted task U to agent \mathcal{A}

2: Initialize: task\_complete\leftarrow\text{False}

3:repeat

4: Agent \mathcal{A} generates tool call with input args: T(I_{T})

5:// Minimize Phase - Filter sensitive information

6: Filtered arguments: I_{T}^{\prime}\leftarrow F_{I}(I_{T},U,T_{desc})

7:// Tool Execution

8: Execute tool with filtered inputs: O_{T}\leftarrow T(I_{T}^{\prime})

9:// Sanitize Phase - Remove malicious content

10: Sanitized output: O_{T}^{\prime}\leftarrow F_{O}(O_{T},U,I_{T}^{\prime})

11:// Response Generation

12: Return O_{T}^{\prime} to agent \mathcal{A}

13:task\_complete\leftarrow\mathcal{A}.\text{isTaskComplete}(U,O_{T}^{\prime})

14:until\mathcal{A} determines task\_complete=\text{True}

15:Output: Agent provides final response to user

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Setup

We evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed input and output firewalls against IPI attacks using four publicly available benchmarks: AgentDojo (debenedetti2024agentdojo), Agent Security Bench (zhang2025agentASB), InjecAgent (zhan-etal-2024-injecagent) and Tau-Bench (yao2025taubench). The prompts for the tool input and tool output firewalls are given in [Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A2 "Appendix B Minimizer and Sanitizer Prompts ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?").

(1) AgentDojo: AgentDojo (debenedetti2024agentdojo) is a widely adopted benchmark for evaluating security in tool-augmented agents. It comprises four task suites: banking, slack, travel, and workspace, spanning a total of 949 security evaluations. The benchmark supports four baseline defenses: spotlighting, repeat-user-prompt and prompt injection detector (PI-detector). We evaluate using the important_instruction attack.

(2) Agent Security Bench (ASB): It is a recent benchmark designed to test agent robustness in a minimum two-stage tool-use setting. Each user task is decomposed into two sub-tasks by the agent, which also selects a subset of tools for each sub-task. ASB includes 400 security evaluations and supports three baseline defenses: delimiters defense, instructional prevention, and sandwich defense. We report results under the Combined attack, which composes all ASB attack types–naive, escape-character, fake completion, and context-ignoring–into a single prompt zhang2025agentASB.

(3) InjecAgent: We also evaluate on InjecAgent (zhan-etal-2024-injecagent), which targets IPI attacks originating from malicious tools. Attacks are designed to cause either direct harm (DH) to the user or data stealing (DS) of private information. The benchmark consists of simulated (pre-determined) tool calls containing either basic or enhanced injection attacks. As all tool calls are static, InjecAgent does not support utility evaluation.

(4) Tau-bench:\tau-bench suite (yao2025taubench) is a benchmark designed to evaluate tool-use agents on dynamic conversational tasks. As \tau-bench is designed primarily for utility evaluation, we augment it with data-stealing attacks using the DoomArena framework (boisvert2025doomarena). Since \tau-bench is primarily designed to assess agentic performance on real-world domains, it is well suited for studying utility–security trade-offs under challenging tasks.

### 4.2 Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate three metrics:

*   •
Benign Utility (BU): Fraction of user tasks solved in the absence of attacks.

*   •
Utility under Attack (UA): Fraction of user tasks solved when injection content is present.

*   •
Attack Success Rate (ASR): Fraction of tasks under attack in which the agent follows injected instructions or triggers unsafe behavior. Safe refusal or ignoring the injection counts as ASR =0.

## 5 Results

We are interested in two key tradeoffs. (a) ASR vs BU: It is important for a defense to not be over-strict and reduce the utility even in the absence of an attack. An ideal defense should maintain the original BU of the agent. (b) ASR vs UA: It highlights how effective a defense is against attacks and whether it impacts utility under attack. An ideal defense should have 0 ASR while maintaining UA the same as BU.

Lower ASR and higher utility are desired. Our proposed defense Sanitizer achieves the best ASR-BU and ASR-UA Tradeoff. Note: Defenses marked with * are non-replicable baselines.

##### AgentDojo.

We compare three firewall defenses: Minimizer, Sanitizer, and Combined, against prior methods including Melon, PromptArmor and CaMeL. 2 2 2 Since we were unable to completely reproduce their results, we report their published results in such cases. From [Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#S5.T1 "In 5 Results ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?"), we see that the Minimizer significantly reduces ASR. Upon qualitatively analyzing the results, we notice that it mainly helps in cases where the attacker attempts to exfiltrate PII information or other extraneous data not essential to the user task. However, since it aggressively redacts input arguments, it can also remove information that is important for task success, leading to a drop in utility. In contrast, the Sanitizer alone is highly effective, achieving 0 ASR across all models while preserving high utility. When both defenses are applied together (combined), the ASR remains 0, but the utility is slightly lower than with the Sanitizer alone due to the Minimizer’s redactions. Compared to the system-level defense baseline (CaMeL), our Sanitizer alone achieves the same ASR (negligible difference) while maintaining much higher utility (BU and UA), demonstrating its strength as a robust defense. Detailed results across different suites and more models are shown in [Appendix C](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A3 "Appendix C Results ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?"), Tables [10](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A3.T10 "Table 10 ‣ C.1 AgentDojo ‣ Appendix C Results ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?") to [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A3.T12 "Table 12 ‣ C.1 AgentDojo ‣ Appendix C Results ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?").

##### Agent Security Bench.

In ASB, since there are no input arguments to the tools, we only use the Sanitizer. The residual non-zero ASR is because several attack-tools perform truly benign operations or return benign-looking outputs, so the Sanitizer (by design) does not flag them, yet ASB counts any invocation of these tools as an attack success. ## 6 Analysis

Generalization to other LLM Backbones.  We report results for Llama 3.3-70B (llama2024modelcards), Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-32B (yang2025qwen3) on all four benchmarks (see [Table 10](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A3.T10 "In C.1 AgentDojo ‣ Appendix C Results ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?") to [20](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A3.T20 "Table 20 ‣ C.4 Tau Bench ‣ Appendix C Results ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?") in [Appendix C](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A3 "Appendix C Results ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")). Across these backbones, we observe the same trade-off: stronger models achieve higher absolute utility, but in all cases, the firewalls significantly reduce ASR while maintaining high benign and under attack utility. Note: It is important to note that the prompt to the Sanitizer is not tuned per model or benchmark, yet the defense remains effective, suggesting that it is not specific to an LLM or a particular benchmark. This demonstrates its robustness and generalizability to diverse attacks.

Data Contamination.  As detailed in [Section H.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A8.SS1 "H.1 Data Contamination ‣ Appendix H Additional Analysis ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?"), benchmark releases postdate the models’ knowledge cut-off dates, ruling out data contamination. To further substantiate this, we use GPT-4o (cut-off: October 2023) as the primary agent and replace the Sanitizer with older models such as GPT-4-turbo (cut-off: April 2024) and Llama 3.3 70B (cut-off: December 2023) and evaluate on AgentDojo (released: June 2024) (Table [22](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A8.T22 "Table 22 ‣ H.2 Replacement of Sanitizer ‣ Appendix H Additional Analysis ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?") in [Section H.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A8.SS2 "H.2 Replacement of Sanitizer ‣ Appendix H Additional Analysis ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")). Across all Sanitizer variants, the ASR remains close to 0, showing that our defense continues to block unseen attacks. This indicates that the effectiveness of our method stems from a generalizable prompting structure rather than contamination. Please refer to [Section H.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A8.SS2 "H.2 Replacement of Sanitizer ‣ Appendix H Additional Analysis ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?") for further details.

Latency Analysis.  We evaluate the time required by different LLM backbones to complete the evaluation, both with and without attack (see [Table 23](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A8.T23 "In H.3 Latency Analysis ‣ Appendix H Additional Analysis ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")). We compare our firewall defense with CaMeL, a state-of-the-art system-level defense on two suites (banking and Slack) of AgentDojo. CaMeL requires 8,417.25 seconds to solve the tool-knowledge attack runs, while our firewall needs only 3,347.93 seconds, achieving a 2.5\times speed-up (see [Table 24](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A8.T24 "In H.3 Latency Analysis ‣ Appendix H Additional Analysis ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")). This demonstrates the efficiency of our approach. Additionally, our firewall defense achieves a competitive security-utility tradeoff, with significantly lower latency compared to other defenses. Please refer to [Section H.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A8.SS3 "H.3 Latency Analysis ‣ Appendix H Additional Analysis ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?") for further details.

Token Overhead Analysis.  We evaluate the token efficiency of different defenses on AgentDojo using GPT-4o as the backbone model. As shown in [Table 25](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A8.T25 "In H.4 Tokens Analysis ‣ Appendix H Additional Analysis ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?"), CaMeL incurs the highest token cost, requiring 2.82\times more input tokens and 2.73\times more output tokens than the baseline (no defense). In contrast, the Sanitizer defense significantly reduces token usage, requiring only 0.51\times the input tokens and 2.67\times the output tokens compared to the baseline, offering similar or better security with far lower prompt overhead as shown in the [Table 25](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A8.T25 "In H.4 Tokens Analysis ‣ Appendix H Additional Analysis ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?"). For input tokens, Sanitizer reduces the mean from 13,463 to 2,036 (\approx 6.6\times fewer tokens), and the median from 3,643 to 1,030 (\approx 3.5\times reduction) as compared to CaMeL. Output token usage is comparable to CaMeL (mean 1,843 vs. 1,675), achieving strong security with much lower prompt overhead.

False Positive and Negative Analysis.  We present the misclassification statistics (FNR, FPR, precision and accuracy) for the Sanitizer in [Table 26](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A8.T26 "In H.5 False Positive and Negative Analysis ‣ Appendix H Additional Analysis ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?"). The Sanitizer demonstrates extremely reliable behavior, with a recall of 99.53%, meaning it misses only 0.47% of attacks, while maintaining 100% precision, indicating zero false positives. This near-perfect recall and absence of false positives support our claim that the Tool-Output Firewall is both effective and precise. These results highlight the strong reliability of the Sanitizer in distinguishing attacks from benign responses.

Cases where Sanitizer degraded utility.  We conducted a qualitative investigation into the utility degradation under benign settings when using the Sanitizer. Across seeds, we identified only 9 specific user tasks where the BU dropped for at least 2 out of 3 seeds. The causes of utility failure were categorized as follows: (a)Agent mistake: the Sanitizer retained the necessary information, but the agent failed to complete the task successfully, unrelated to any Sanitizer behavior. (b)Sanitizer redaction: the Sanitizer filtered out content necessary for task completion, leading to a failure. Out of the 9 cases, 7 were due to agent mistakes, while only 2 were attributed to sanitizer redaction. This breakdown indicates that most of the BU degradation stems from agent variability, rather than overly aggressive redaction by the Sanitizer. Please refer to [Section H.6](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A8.SS6 "H.6 Qualitative Analysis ‣ Appendix H Additional Analysis ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?") for examples of such cases.

Uncertainty ablation. To probe _why_ the firewall succeeds, we measure whether prompt injections push the model into a different confidence regime. Concretely, we compute output uncertainty (token entropy over the top-5 token probabilities at each step) and perplexity for the Sanitizer’s (GPT-4o) outputs under clean versus injected tool outputs (across 3 seeds) on the Slack suite of AgentDojo. We find that both metrics remain essentially unchanged: entropy is 0.318\pm 0.001 (clean) versus 0.321\pm 0.001 (injected), and perplexity is 1.221\pm 0.002 (clean) versus 1.215\pm 0.002 (injected). The near-identical uncertainty scores indicate that injections do not induce abnormal confidence shifts or increased uncertainty. This is consistent with the fact that many existing injections are easily identifiable (e.g., via explicit formatting cues such as special tags). These uncertainty metrics, combined with our results on four benchmarks, demonstrate that the firewall not only performs effectively but operates with high confidence, making it reliable for real-world deployment.

## 7 Assessment of Current Benchmarks

While evaluating the firewall defense on already existing benchmarks, we systematically identified several critical limitations in them. Below, we summarize these limitations, propose targeted fixes and report detailed results on these revised benchmarks (detailed results in [Appendix C](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A3 "Appendix C Results ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")). Finally, we provide guidelines for future research.

### 7.1 AgentDojo

##### Issue 1: Injection vectors overwrite task-critical content.

In several tasks, IPIs are implemented by _replacing_ environment content that is essential for completing the task (e.g., overwriting the items and payment amounts in a bill to be paid; see [Section D.1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A4.SS1.SSS1 "D.1.1 Injection vectors overwrite task-critical content ‣ D.1 AgentDojo ‣ Appendix D Limitations of Current Benchmarks ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")). This design makes the task unsolvable regardless of whether the agent disregards the malicious instruction. As a result, the observed utility drop primarily arises from data replacement rather than successful IPI influence. This confounds comparisons across defenses and often induces ceiling effects. Hence, for a faithful evaluation, IPIs should preserve task-critical signals, ensuring that measured failures are attributed to agentic failures rather than missing essential task information.

Issue 2: Brittle utility metrics mis-score success. Another issue with AgentDojo arises from overly rigid utility evaluation metrics that fail to capture the semantic goal of the task. In particular, some metrics hinge on strict counters or exact state deltas, and thus incorrectly penalize cases where the task is successfully completed but additional (possibly attack-induced) events occur. For instance, in the Slack suite, the agent is asked to summarize an article and send it to a specific user (see [Section D.1.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A4.SS1.SSS2 "D.1.2 Brittle utility checks mis-score success ‣ D.1 AgentDojo ‣ Appendix D Limitations of Current Benchmarks ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")). The utility function evaluates success by checking whether the target user’s inbox contains exactly one more message than before the task began. This strict cardinality check fails if the agent, due to either a valid reasoning path or a mild injection side effect, sends more than one message, even if one of them correctly fulfills the task. Such scoring rules disregard successful task completion whenever any auxiliary behavior occurs, thereby systematically underestimating utility.

Our fixes improved the utility under attack. We found multiple such cases where fixing the injection vector placements and utility functions to assess goal achievement based on content rather than strict state deltas led to significant improvements (>18\%) as shown in Table [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#S7.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 7.2 Agent Security Bench ‣ 7 Assessment of Current Benchmarks ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?").

### 7.2 Agent Security Bench

ASB evaluates agent robustness by structuring each task into a two-stage workflow. For every user query, the agent is first prompted to decompose the task into two sub-tasks and select an appropriate subset of tools from a provided tool set for each sub-task. Tool calls are executed based on these selections, and the raw outputs of all invoked tools are concatenated and returned to the agent. This setup aims to simulate a multi-step tool-augmented reasoning process.

Issue 1: Forced attack-tool injection inflates ASR. A critical limitation of ASB is that it forcibly injects attacker-controlled tools (“attack-tools”) into each sub-task’s tool subset, even when the agent did not select them. Since ASB defines attack success as the agent invoking these tools, this forced inclusion directly inflates ASR. This setup violates the core premise of IPI evaluation: attacks should succeed by manipulating the agent via prompt injections in untrusted content, not by externally overriding tool-selection or execution trajectories (greshake2023not).

Issue 2: Utility Evaluation is Poorly Structured. Another major limitation of ASB is its coarse and static method for evaluating task success. Tool responses are hardcoded and independent of input arguments, so evaluation reduces to checking whether the agent invoked a predefined set of tools. This can be gamed: some models, such as LLaMA 70B, adopt a brute-force strategy by calling all available tools at every planning step, yielding perfect utility scores (=1) regardless of whether the task was completed meaningfully. If an attacker tool is invoked at any point, the utility score is directly assigned zero, regardless of whether the agent still completed the user-task, thereby ignoring task semantics. Moreover, many real-world tasks require tools to be used in a specific sequence (e.g., calling get_balance() before make_transaction() in banking), but ASB neither enforces nor evaluates such a structure, allowing agents to obtain full utility even by calling the right tools in the wrong order. Consequently, ASB fails to capture whether the agent actually completed the user-task as intended, limiting its usefulness for measuring real-world performance.

Table 5: Revised benchmark results for GPT-4o with no defense.

Our fixes correct the ASR. When we let the agent freely select tools from the full tool set (which still includes attack-tools) without forced injection, the ASR drops sharply from 70% to 9.25%, an almost 8\times reduction (Table [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#S7.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 7.2 Agent Security Bench ‣ 7 Assessment of Current Benchmarks ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")). This shows that most of the original “attack success” arises from benchmark-induced control-flow manipulation rather than agent vulnerability, leading to an inflated ASR and misleading conclusions about robustness. An example of this issue and its correction has been provided in [Section D.2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A4.SS2.SSS1 "D.2.1 Forced Attack-Tool Injection Distorts ASR ‣ D.2 Agent Security Bench ‣ Appendix D Limitations of Current Benchmarks ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?").

### 7.3 InjecAgent

A primary metric for any agent evaluation is BU, which measures how useful the agent is even in the absence of attacks. In the presence of attacks, it is crucial to also measure the UA along with the ASR to characterize the security–utility tradeoff. InjecAgent reports the ASR, but provides no utility metric. This makes it impossible to measure either the BU or UA and, therefore, to assess the utility impact of defenses. Further, its attacks are overly simplistic and are becoming increasingly obsolete in the face of newer LLM-agents.

## 8 Cascade Attack Strategy

Table 6: Cascade attack breakdown. Stage columns report the number of additional successful attacks found at each stage in the Slack suite of AgentDojo, and the cumulative success rate.

Motivation.  While our firewalls robustly defend against current IPI attacks, they also expose limitations of existing benchmarks: many attack strategies are rigid, context-agnostic, or depend on fixed trigger phrases, and thus can overestimate defense effectiveness and obscure true failure modes. To address this gap, we develop a cascade attack strategy to show that existing LLM-based prompt injection detection baselines like PromptArmor can be compromised, but the Sanitizer still demonstrates strong robustness.

Setup.  We evaluate defenses with a three-stage approach that increases attack complexity only when earlier stages fail. (a) Stage 1 applies existing attacks from AgentDojo (we use tool_knowledge attack). While effective against undefended agents, these attacks are largely mitigated by LLM-based detection defenses that target hidden instructions. (b) Stage 2 introduces seven second-order semantic templates that rely on deception rather than surface-form instruction patterns. The goal is to present the actual injection as benign, trusted-looking metadata, sometimes alongside an overtly malicious decoy. (c) Stage 3 uses an LLM-based adaptive attacker that iteratively refines injections over 10 iterations using examples of past successes and failures and defense feedback, along with the defense knowledge. Further details are provided in [Appendix E](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A5 "Appendix E Cascade Attack Strategy ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?").

Results.  Existing IPI benchmarks predominantly use rigid, format-driven attacks (e.g., fixed tags or formatting), which can make LLM-based defenses appear stronger than they are in practice. [Table 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#S8.T6 "In 8 Cascade Attack Strategy ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?") reports per-stage and cumulative ASR under our cascade attack strategy. As expected, Stage 1 formatted injections are largely handled by LLM-based baselines. In Stage 2, which introduces second-order semantic templates, PromptArmor is easily fooled by our semantic templates, resulting in a large increase in ASR, whereas the Sanitizer remains comparatively robust with only a modest ASR increase. We attribute this gap to the different design strategies: PromptArmor-style defenses use an LLM to _detect_ and filter suspected injections (using fuzzy logic), while our Sanitizer _regenerates_ the tool call output without the injection and explicitly explains removals, making it less dependent on surface-form triggers. Notably, we observe non-trivial ASR for the Sanitizer only in Stage 3, where we employ defense-aware adaptive attacks. Even with this much stronger and costlier attacker, successes remain limited, showing that bypassing the Sanitizer demands significantly greater attacker capability. Overall, these findings support including defense-aware adaptive attacks as a standard protocol for evaluating prompt-injection robustness.

## 9 Discussion and Conclusion

Guidelines for Future Benchmarks.  Based on our extensive analysis, we propose the following guidelines for designing reliable prompt injection benchmarks:

*   •
Benchmarks should not forcibly inject tools or override the agent’s planning decisions, as this distorts ASR.

*   •
Injection vectors should be inserted without overwriting key inputs or environmental context needed for task completion. Otherwise, any utility drop may reflect data deletion, not agentic failure.

*   •
Utility should be measured using task-specific checks that reflect semantic goal completion, including correct tool sequencing, input-output flow, and partial success.

*   •
Every benchmark should provide metrics for BU, ASR and UA to study the trade-off between security and utility.

*   •
Benchmarks must include stronger and more adaptive attacks as we show in Section [8](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#S8 "8 Cascade Attack Strategy ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?"). Currently implemented attacks are saturated and weak, which may fail to adequately distinguish between various defenses.

Conclusion.  Firewalls can achieve perfect security across all publicly available benchmarks, matching or exceeding the performance of complex defenses. This suggests that firewalls are a very strong baseline defense. Our investigation also uncovers several limitations in current evaluation frameworks. Going forward, we do not think InjecAgent (no utility) or ASB numbers (lack of arguments, unnatural tool calling setting) should be used for future AI security evaluations. We believe AgentDojo played an instrumental role in bootstrapping the IPI field, but requires fixes. We suggest evaluating using our revised versions instead (AgentDojo-Revised) and report the Sanitizer as a simple but effective baseline. Moreover, all these benchmarks need stronger, more adaptive attack strategies. To this end, we propose a cascade attack strategy that bypasses all existing defenses, with the Sanitizer being the least compromised.

## 10 Impact statement

As is the case for other defenses, using firewalls has the potential to increase the security of tool-calling agents against prompt injections. However, like most indirect prompt injection defenses, the firewalls we have presented here assume that the user task is fully trusted, and that the tools themselves are secure (apart from serving poisoned data during attack). We should be wary that there is a risk that developers will overrely on security defenses without fully understanding the conditions under which they are effective and their limitations. Finally, the firewall steps may require additional API calls, potentially increasing the risk of leaking important or private data.

## Appendix E Cascade Attack Strategy

This section details our _cascade attack_ evaluation, designed to avoid overly optimistic robustness estimates from rigid, format-driven prompt-injection attacks. Concretely, many existing IPI attacks rely on fixed tags, trigger phrases, or easily recognizable instruction patterns; such attacks can be handled by LLM-based detectors while failing to reveal semantic failure modes. Our cascade therefore escalates attacker capability only when a defense blocks earlier stages, providing a more informative and defense-sensitive robustness measurement.

##### Threat model and cascade structure.

Each trial begins with a existing prompt-injection attack. If the defense blocks the attempt (e.g., via sanitization, refusal, or other failure signal), the attacker escalates to the next stage. Importantly, Stage 2 and Stage 3 are only invoked on instances not already succeeded in earlier stages.

##### Stage 1: Direct formatted injections.

Stage 1 uses some attack from AgentDojo (in our case, we use tool_knowledge attack) that explicitly encode tool-oriented instructions using standard injection formatting. This stage captures the dominant style of attacks in many current benchmarks and serves as a low-cost baseline attacker.

##### Stage 2: Second-order semantic templates.

When Stage 1 fails, Stage 2 applies semantic deception strategies that do not rely on the same surface-form instruction patterns. We instantiate seven template families (Table [21](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#A5.T21 "Table 21 ‣ Key observations. ‣ Appendix E Cascade Attack Strategy ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?")), including variants that (i) present the payload in trusted-looking wrappers (e.g., system-style tags or provenance cues) and (ii) optionally include a high-salience decoy designed to concentrate the defense on an easily identifiable malicious span while the true payload is framed as routine metadata. This stage targets brittleness in defenses that primarily detect instruction-like strings or canonical injection formats.

##### Stage 3: Defense-aware adaptive refinement.

When Stage 2 fails, Stage 3 uses an LLM-based adaptive attacker that performs 10 mutation rounds. The mutator conditions on (i) examples of successful and failed attacks from earlier stages, (ii) explicit feedback from the defense, and (iii) defense knowledge. This operationalizes a defense-aware adaptive threat model: rather than sampling static prompts, the attacker iteratively searches for prompts that preserve injection efficacy while evading the specific defense.

##### Key observations.

[Table 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05244v2#S8.T6 "In 8 Cascade Attack Strategy ‣ Indirect Prompt Injections: Are Firewalls All You Need, or Stronger Benchmarks?") reports per-stage and cumulative ASR under our cascade attack strategy. As expected, Stage 1 formatted injections are largely handled by LLM-based baselines. In Stage 2, which introduces second-order semantic templates, PromptArmor is easily fooled by our semantic templates, resulting in a large ASR improvement, whereas the Sanitizer remains comparatively robust with only a modest increase. We attribute this gap to the different designs strategies: PromptArmor-style defenses use an LLM to _detect_ and filter suspected injections (using fuzzy logic), while our Sanitizer _regenerates_ the tool call output without the injection and explicitly explains removals, making it less dependent on surface-form triggers. Notably, we observe non-trivial ASR for the Sanitizer only in Stage 3, where we employ a defense-aware adaptive attacks. Even with this much stronger and costlier attacker, successes remain limited, showing that bypassing the Sanitizer demands significantly greater attacker capability. Overall, these findings support including defense-aware adaptive attacks as a standard protocol for evaluating prompt-injection robustness.

Overall, these results suggest that robustness evaluations should not rely solely on static or format-driven attacks. Defense-aware adaptive attacks (such as Stage 3) should be included as a standard protocol for measuring robustness against prompt injection, since they better reflect an attacker optimizing against the deployed defense.
